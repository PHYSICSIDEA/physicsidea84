Tuan Pham

I’m a statistical physicist. I work on large systems whose interactions are not fixed: couplings that fluctuate, adapt, or co-evolve with the states they connect. I ask what this does to order, robustness and irreversibility, in neural and gene-regulatory networks and in societies.

Research fellow at the Dutch Institute for Emergent Phenomena, University of Amsterdam. Also affiliated with the Institute for Advanced Study, Amsterdam, and the Complexity Science Hub Vienna.

Research

A thread runs through most of my work: fast degrees of freedom coupled through interactions that change on their own, slower or faster, timescale. Neural activity and synapses, phenotypes and genotypes, opinions and social ties. Three directions grow out of it.

Irreversibility in non-reciprocal networks

When unit i acts on j differently than j acts on i, a network cannot relax to equilibrium. I use dynamical mean-field theory to derive exact entropy production rates for such networks: how irreversibility marks the onset of chaos, and how it depends on the timescale on which the couplings themselves fluctuate.

    Adaptation across timescales

    Fast variables such as gene expression or signal responses, coupled to parameters that adapt slowly, like the gene-regulatory network itself. I extend path-integral (MSRDJ) and replica methods to these two-timescale problems, and use them to explain when robust phenotypes emerge in evolution, and how cells adapt to fluctuating signals in a way that resembles learning.

      Collective social dynamics

      Opinions and relationships co-evolve. With spin-glass and voter-type models I study why societies fragment into cohesive, mutually hostile groups, why polarization can grow as societies become more connected, what sets the size of social groups, and how symmetric preferences can still tip a city into segregation, and I test these ideas against data and experiments. This is the focus of my fellowship on polarisation, segregation and inequality.

        Methods I use and develop: dynamical mean-field theory with MSRDJ path integrals, replica theory, the cavity method and belief propagation, and the spectral theory of sparse random graphs (Phys. Rev. E 2024). Earlier work covers stochastic processes, from noise-induced transitions to fractional kinetics.

          Reading notes

          Papers I find interesting, with a few lines on why.

          ▸ RNNs incrementally evolving on an equilibrium manifold: a panacea for vanishing and exploding gradients? Anil Kag, Ziming Zhang, Venkatesh Saligrama. ICLR 2020

          This paper’s key insight is how to exploit effective equilibrium dynamics of the state vector as well as the discrete samples by virtue of the combination of “inherent” low pass filtering, as encoded by the decay term, and slowly time varying input. The implementation is done by including hidden variable whose dynamics are identical to that of visible ones but crucially happen on different timescale.

          This idea is tightly related to cell differentiation under epigenetic modification. The latter is modeled by epigenetic parameters that do not necessarily obey the same dynamics as that of genes.
